ZKNOT / Edge autonomy integrity

Proving what an autonomous vehicle launched with, and what it did

Concept of operations & bench work · Fall 2026

A drone that flies dark (no command link, no telemetry) is very hard to hack in flight. The realistic threat is that it is compromised on the ground and acts in flight. We designed a small, independent board that checks the vehicle's software before arming, listens during the mission, asks for the companion computer to be cut out if the vehicle leaves its mission, and signs every step so someone else can check afterward whether it was hacked or simply malfunctioned.

Where this stands. We wrote this concept in fall 2026 for an Air Force STTR topic on cyber resiliency for fog and edge computing devices. We did not submit a proposal. What's below is the concept and the bench work we did on lab hardware. None of it has been tested on a vehicle, and we say where each result came from.

The problem

A typical small UAS pairs a flight controller (for example PX4) with a companion computer (for example an NVIDIA Orin running Ubuntu and ROS2) that runs the autonomy. That companion computer is a full Linux system with a large software supply chain: packages, drivers and AI models that change through normal maintenance, often from a laptop in the field.

That leaves two questions most programs can't answer with evidence today:

  • Before launch: is the software on this vehicle exactly what was approved?
  • After an incident: was the vehicle hacked, or did it malfunction? And can someone outside the team check the answer?

Concept of operations: four parts

The board sits beside the flight stack. It never flies or navigates the vehicle.

PartWhenWhat it doesWhat it doesn't do
GateOn the ground, before armingChecks the companion computer's software and the flight controller's firmware and safety parameters (return-to-home, geofence, home point) against what was declared. A mismatch means the vehicle doesn't arm.It can't catch a bad package that is part of the approved image. It proves the software is what was approved, nothing more.
WatchIn flightListens only. It compares what the companion computer commands with its own copy of the mission: corridor, speed, message rate, number of publishers, radio silence. It publishes nothing to the vehicle's network.It doesn't run autonomy or navigate. It can't see an attack that keeps the vehicle inside its corridor.
RespondOn a confirmed alarmAsks for the companion computer to be cut off from control through one narrow interface. What the flight controller then does (hold, continue the preloaded route, return, land) is up to the vehicle maker.It doesn't try to restore the companion computer in flight. Recovery happens on the ground.
EvidenceThroughoutSigns each verdict, so after the flight a third party can check offline what was observed, and when.It doesn't prove the cause. It proves what was seen and when.

Threats it is built for

  • Supply chain, caught before flight. Firmware, parameters or companion software that don't match the declared baseline. The Gate stops the arm.
  • A dormant implant inside approved software. A poisoned dependency, driver or model gets in through normal maintenance and passes the Gate because it is part of the approved image. It stays quiet, then diverts the vehicle when triggered by a time, a place or a target. Only the Watch can catch it, and only if the effect shows outside the companion computer.
  • Deception. Decoys, adversarial inputs or GPS spoofing that fool the autonomy without compromising it. Same rule: caught only if the vehicle leaves its mission envelope.

Why the signed records matter: if the Gate signed that the approved image was loaded, and the Watch then signed that the vehicle left its envelope, the evidence points at the approved software, meaning the supply chain, not a random fault. That answers "hacked or malfunctioned?" from records anyone can check.

Bench results (lab hardware, not a vehicle)

Pre-flight gate · Oct–Nov 2026
An STM32 microcontroller stood in for a flight controller. Our gate held it in reset, read its firmware, compared it with a declared image and signed a verdict with a hardware-backed key. Each verdict can be checked offline back to the chip maker's root certificate. A modified firmware image produced a signed FAIL, and the target stayed held, so it never ran. Tampered and mismatched records failed verification.

In-flight watch · 2026-11-11
Two lab computers: one stood in for the companion computer and published ROS2 setpoints along a survey route. A separate receive-only box judged them against the mission.

RunResult

How tight that corridor can be on a real vehicle, in wind and with GPS error, has to be measured in flight.

What we don't claim

  • Runtime monitors for autonomy are not new (Simplex, DARPA HACMS, NASA's R2U2 and Copilot, ASTM F3269). What we think is less common is the signed, independently checkable record of what was loaded and what was observed.
  • The board is itself an attack surface. That's why it only listens, has a single output, and keeps its own copy of the mission.
  • Out of scope: protecting the flight controller in flight, a second flight stack, anti-capture, EW and EMP hardening, and GPS-spoof detection on its own.

Where this fits for a vehicle maker

The Gate and the Evidence parts don't depend on flight at all. They answer a question programs and auditors already ask: prove what firmware and parts are on this delivered unit.

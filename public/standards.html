No device makes an organisation compliant. Auditors don't accept hardware — they accept records, and they ask the same four questions of every one.

Every ZKNOT product writes to one public verification rail, and every record on it carries an assurance tier that declares what it proves and what it does not. That second half is the unusual part, and it is the half an auditor actually needs.

The problem

Four questions an auditor asks of any record.

Frameworks differ enormously in scope and wording, but the evidentiary core repeats. A paper log, a spreadsheet, and a database row all fail in the same places: they are assertions by the party being audited, and nothing outside that party can test them.

01
What exactly happened?
Not a summary written afterwards. The specific bytes — the file, the reading, the image — fixed at the moment of the event, so that "the thing we attested" cannot quietly become a different thing.
02
What asserted it, and is that thing identifiable?
A signature is only as meaningful as the identity behind the key. The honest question is not "is it signed" but "whose key, and who vouches for that key — you, or someone else?"
03
Has it changed since?
Records are hash-chained: each entry commits to the one before it. A record altered after the fact no longer matches the chain, and the mismatch is visible to anyone checking — not only to the vendor.
04
Can someone outside check it?
Verification runs in the reader's own browser against a public endpoint. An auditor, a counterparty, or an opposing expert can check a record without an account, without asking you, and without taking ZKNOT's word for the signature math.
The assurance ladder

Every record declares its own tier — including the limits.

Most security vendors publish what their product proves. The tier text below is what the verifier shows a reader, verbatim, and each rung carries a “does not prove” line. A weak record is not dressed up as a strong one, which means evidence you present is evidence you can defend when someone pushes on it.

SELF-ASSERTED
Rung 1
Proves
A signature exists that verifies against the public key carried in this record.
Does not prove
Nothing about who holds that key. The owner is whoever the record claims; no independent party vouches for it.
REGISTRY-ASSERTED
Rung 2
Proves
The record was signed by ZKNOT's registry key. The registry vouches that this registration exists and has not been altered.
Does not prove
That an independent device with its own secure element signed it. It is not a device-held key and not a human-presence event.
KEY-REGISTERED
Rung 3
Proves
The key that signed this is on file in the ZKNOT registry and was generated inside a secure element it has never left. The registry vouches for the key.
Does not prove
That ZKNOT vouches for the device holding it. The key is on file; the machine around it is not attested, and a modified unit signs with the same registered key.
REGISTERED
Rung 4
Proves
The signing device's own keypair is on file in the ZKNOT registry.
Does not prove
An X.509 CA certificate chain — none was verified. Registration vouches for the key; what surrounds the key is a property of the specific product, stated in that product's own claims and not by this rung.
PUBLISHING-WITNESS
Rung 5
Proves
The signature was produced by the secure element inside a designated witness device whose key ZKNOT vouches for. It commits to the SHA-256 of the attested bytes, so it also fixes the content: change one byte and the record no longer matches.
Does not prove
That any person was present, was shown the content, or approved anything. It is a witness, not a gate — nothing about who wrote the content, whether it is true, or what may be done with it.

Our own yardsticks

The standards we hold ourselves against.

We maintain an internal map of the standard or regulation each product is measured against, so design decisions are argued against a published yardstick rather than our own taste. Measuring against a standard is not a certification, and nothing below is a claim of conformance or of third-party assessment. Where a product carries a formal obligation, that is a matter of our paperwork, not a feature of yours.

IETF RFC 9334
Remote attestation roles — attester, verifier, relying party. We use its vocabulary instead of inventing our own, so our records can be reasoned about with published terms.
IETF RFC 9162
The verifiable-log model behind certificate transparency. The yardstick for the property we care most about: that a vendor cannot quietly rewrite its own history.
IETF RFC 3161
Time-stamp structure. The shape of "this hash existed at this time" that courts and reviewers already recognise.
IETF RFC 8785
JSON canonicalisation. A hash must cover stored bytes, never a value re-rendered at read time — a lesson we learned the expensive way and wrote down.
ISO 22383
Performance criteria for authentication solutions. The frame for stating what a physical authentication feature resists, instead of calling it "secure".
NIST SP 800-57
Key lifecycle. In particular the distinction between a retired key and a compromised one, which a verifier has to be able to express.
Read this before you cite us

What we do not claim.

This list exists because the fastest way to damage your audit is to carry a vendor's overstatement into it. If a ZKNOT surface ever claims more than this page allows, the surface is wrong — write to us and we will correct it.

  • We are not a certification body, testing laboratory, or notified body. We do not assess, accredit, or certify you or anyone else.
  • No ZKNOT product makes any organisation compliant with any framework. Controls are satisfied by your processes and judged by your assessor. We supply evidence that enters that judgement.
  • No product is certified to any standard named on this page. These are yardsticks we measure ourselves against, not certifications we hold.
  • No FIPS claim of any kind is made for any shipping product. A cryptographic module claim requires a live CMVP validation; we will say so by certificate number if that ever changes.
  • Tamper-evident, never tamper-proof. A seal is designed to reveal interference. It does not prevent it, and it says nothing about the contents it seals.
  • A signature is not a statement that a human was present unless the tier says so. Registration vouches for a key; it does not attest the machine around it or anyone standing near it.
  • Registry-vouched is not independently anchored. Where the only party vouching for a key's identity is ZKNOT, the verifier says exactly that, in those words.
  • We do not hold your data to make verification work. A verifier re-checks signature math in the reader's browser; you can recompute the hash of your own copy yourself.
